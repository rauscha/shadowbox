Least squares

Before you trust any line through any scatter, you should get to choose one by hand and feel what “best” costs.

Every regression figure in every paper is the output of a machine that was handed a scatter of points and a definition of best, and returned a line. This page opens that machine. You will drive it by hand first, see the definition it optimizes drawn as literal ink on the page, and then watch the whole search collapse into geometry — a bowl with a bottom. At the end you will run it on four hundred real births, where the machine works perfectly and the more interesting question — should it? — quietly appears.

Start from zero: lines and errors

A line is two decisions. The slope is how much the prediction rises for each unit you move right — “about 190 extra grams per extra week,” say. The intercept is where the line sits when the horizontal axis reads zero: raise it and the whole line moves up without tilting; it anchors the line’s height.

Once you have a line, it makes a promise at every point: for this x, I predict this y. The error of that promise, called the residual, is the vertical gap between the promise and what actually happened — how far the real point sits above or below the line. A line fits well when its gaps are collectively small. The entire question of this page is what “collectively” should mean.

The squares are not a metaphor

Below, twelve synthetic points and a line you control. Drag the line’s body to slide it; drag its round handles to tilt it; drag the points themselves if you want to make trouble. Each vertical gap has been squared — drawn as an actual square whose side is the gap. The dots filling each square are laid on a fixed grid, so bigger squares carry more dots, and the readout in the corner is the total.

Try to make the total small. That instinct you are using — shrink the big squares first, tolerate several small ones — is the definition. Least squares is nothing more than the line that spends the least ink. Squaring is why one huge gap costs more than four small ones: a gap twice as large buys a square four times the area. The fit fears outliers because you drew the error as an area, and areas grow fast.

Every line is a point somewhere else

Your line has just two knobs — slope and intercept. So the whole universe of candidate lines is a two-dimensional plane: slope across, intercept up. Every line you tried above is one point in that plane, and the total ink it spent is a height. Here is that landscape.

The two figures are one object. Drag the line above and this marker moves; drag the marker and the line follows. Darker is less ink, and the rings are contour lines crowding toward a single lowest point. The surface is a bowl — smooth, with no false valleys — so the best line was never really a search through lines at all. It is the bottom of the bowl, and everything rolls there.

Now press absolute error on the first figure. The squares become sticks — gaps counted at face value instead of squared — and the bowl grows a crease along its floor. “Squared” is not a law of nature. It is a choice, with consequences: the smooth bowl gives calculus something to grip, and it is why outliers pull so hard. Absolute error forgives outliers and creases the landscape. Statistics is full of such choices dressed up as defaults.

The bottom can be solved, not searched

A smooth bowl has one bottom, and calculus finds it in closed form. For slope m^ and intercept b^:

m^ = Sxy Sxx b^ = y¯ − m^ x¯

Read it as geometry, not ceremony: the slope is how much x and y vary together (their covariance, Sxy) divided by how much x varies alone (Sxx); the intercept then slides the line through the cloud’s center of mass (x¯, y¯). Those two quantities — covariance and variance — are the entire machine, and rung 2 is about seeing them as shapes.

When the truth is drawn on screen

These twelve points are synthetic: they were manufactured by adding noise to a line I know. Press show the true line above, then resample a few times. The truth holds still; the fit wobbles around it, tugged by each new draw of noise. That wobble is the whole subject of statistical inference — how far a fit can stray from a truth — and here you can see it only because the truth is drawn. Remember the feeling. You are about to lose it.

Four hundred real births

Same instrument, no tricks: 400 births sampled from the 2014 US natality file — gestational age against birthweight. There is no show the truth button now, because nobody has the truth. There is only the machine and its definition of best.

The fitted slope is real knowledge: on the order of two hundred grams per week, which is why a 36-weeker and a 40-weeker are different babies. But drag a single extreme preemie and watch the fit barely move — then look at the left edge, where the relationship visibly bends away from any straight line. Growth is not linear across this range, and the machine does not care.

The machine happily fits a line whether or not a line is the right question. Nothing in the readout will tell you. The next two rungs build the eyes that do.
