Principal components

Principal component analysis is a method for finding the directions a cloud of data actually spreads along, and then describing the data in those directions instead of the ones it happened to be measured in. It is almost always shortened to PCA. The directions it finds are called principal components, and in the linear-algebra telling of the story they are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

Rung 2 drew a cloud as an ellipse with one width, one height and one tilt. PCA is what happens when you take that ellipse seriously and ask which way its long axis points.

Start from zero: projection and spread

To project a point onto a line, drop a perpendicular from the point down to the line and mark where it lands. The line acts like a wall and the point casts a shadow onto it. Every point in a cloud casts one, so a two-dimensional cloud becomes a scatter of marks strung along a single line, and each point has gone from two numbers to one.

Two things about that shadow are worth tracking. How spread out the shadows are along the line, which is a variance in the sense of rung 2. And how far each point had to travel to reach the line, which is a distance; square those and add them up and you have a total error in the sense of rung 1.

One line, two numbers

A cloud has infinitely many lines through it, so PCA needs a rule for picking one, exactly as least squares did. Two different rules suggest themselves, and at first they look like they are asking for different things.

The first rule is to pick the line whose shadows are spread out as much as possible. A line running along the length of the cloud gives shadows that fan out across the whole length. A line running across the cloud crushes them into a clump. Spread-out shadows keep more of the difference between the points.

The second rule is to pick the line the points sit closest to: add up the squared perpendicular distances from every point to the line and make that total as small as possible.

Turn the line below with the angle slider and watch both numbers at once. They move in opposite directions, and not approximately. Their sum never changes.

The sum is fixed because of a right triangle. For any one point, the distance along the line and the distance across to it are the two legs, and the distance from the center of the cloud out to the point is the hypotenuse. Pythagoras forces the two squared legs to add up to a squared hypotenuse that doesn't depend on the angle at all. Add that up over every point and the variance along the line plus the variance across it always equals the total variance of the cloud.

So the two rules are not two rules. Spreading the shadows out as far as possible and pulling the line as close to the points as possible are one instruction approached from two sides, and the angle that satisfies it is PC1. On this cloud PC1 sits at about 34 degrees and carries 2.16 out of a total variance of 2.50, which is 86 percent of it. The direction at right angles to PC1 is PC2, and it gets the remaining 0.34.

Three lines, three questions

Rung 1 had a note in it saying that a residual is measured vertically rather than perpendicular to the line, and that the choice mattered more than it looked. This is where it matters.

Least squares of y on x minimizes vertical error, because it treats x as known and y as the thing being predicted. Least squares of x on y minimizes horizontal error, because it swaps those two roles. PC1 minimizes perpendicular error, because it treats both variables as measurements and has nothing it is trying to predict. One cloud, three lines, and they don't agree.

On this cloud the slopes come out at 0.53 for y on x, 1.07 for x on y and 0.67 for PC1, so the two regressions differ from each other by a factor of two. Which one is correct depends entirely on the question being asked, and none of them is a true line that the other two are failing to reach. The error marks say which question each line answered: vertical combs, horizontal combs, perpendicular combs. Switch the families on and off to see them separately.

Two things are always true here. All three lines pass through the mean of the cloud, which rung 1 showed for the least-squares line and which holds for all of them. And PC1 always lies between the two regressions. Raise the correlation and the three lines converge; lower it and they fan apart. A paper that reports the regression line between two noisy measurements has quietly chosen one of the three.

Same cloud, new axes

Everything so far has been about a single line. PCA uses both directions.

PC1 and PC2 are at right angles to each other, which means they can serve as a new pair of axes. Rewriting every point's coordinates in those axes changes nothing about the data. No point moves relative to any other, no distance between points changes, and the total variance is identical. The only thing that changes is the pair of directions the numbers are quoted in.

Slide below from data coordinates into PC coordinates and watch the covariance matrix in the corner. The off-diagonal entry falls to zero.

That zero is most of what PCA is for. A covariance of zero means the two new coordinates carry no straight-line information about each other, so the tilt that rung 2 spent a whole section on has gone, not because the cloud changed but because it is now being described along its own axes instead of along the ones the measuring equipment happened to use. The two variances left on the diagonal are the eigenvalues, and they are the spread along each new direction.

More than two variables

Two variables can simply be plotted, so PCA has not earned much yet. It earns its keep when there are more variables than a plot can hold.

A routine growth scan measures four things: biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference and femur length, all in millimeters. Four variables give a 4×4 covariance matrix, four principal components and four eigenvalues. The bars below show how the total spread divides among them, with the loadings that say what the first two components are made of.

Read the loadings on PC1 in raw millimeters. Abdominal circumference gets +0.75 and head circumference +0.62, while biparietal diameter gets +0.18 and femur length +0.15. PC1 is being described mostly as abdomen and head.

Now press standardize each variable. The four loadings become +0.50, +0.50, +0.50 and +0.50, weighted essentially equally.

Nothing about the fetuses changed. AC and HC dominated the first version because they are the numerically biggest measurements: abdominal circumference has a variance of about 3,500 mm² where femur length has about 140. PCA maximizes variance, variance is in squared units, and so the variable carrying the largest numbers wins by default. This is rung 2's units trap with consequences attached. There, changing the units changed a number nobody was going to interpret. Here, changing the units changes which variables the method says the data is about.

Standardising first is the usual fix, and it's what a lot of software does without saying so. It isn't automatically the right call. It declares that a millimeter of femur matters as much as a millimeter of abdomen, which is a decision about the biology rather than about the arithmetic.

Why is PC1 so large?

The first component takes just under 98 percent of the variance in raw millimeters, and 97.5 percent standardized. A number that large normally reads as a success, but here it is a sign that one obvious thing is driving every measurement at once.

Every pair of these four measurements correlates above 0.95: BPD with HC at 0.97, HC with AC at 0.96, and so on around all six pairs. The reason is not subtle. These scans span 20 to 40 weeks, and everything about a fetus gets bigger across that range. Gestational age is driving all four measurements at once, so a single everything-is-large direction absorbs almost all of the spread. PC1 here is gestational age wearing a disguise, and it took a 4×4 matrix to rediscover a number already printed at the top of the report.

So what about the 2 percent left over? In raw millimeters PC2 is head against abdomen, HC +0.75 against AC −0.65. That is a tempting story, because head-sparing asymmetric growth is a real phenomenon and it would look exactly like that. But press standardize and PC2 turns into abdomen against everything else, AC +0.78 with BPD −0.51. The same data, one analyst decision apart, tells two different stories about a component holding around one percent of the variance. Neither belongs in a manuscript.

The fix is to take out the driver you already know about. Fit each measurement against gestational age, keep the residual, and standardize it, so that each fetus is described by how large every part is for its dates rather than by how far along it is. Those four columns are what the figure below runs PCA on.

The correlations fall from about 0.96 to about 0.3, and the spread divides 49, 19, 17 and 16 percent instead of 98, 2, 0 and 0. PC1 is still a size component, four positive loadings all near 0.5, but it now means large-for-dates instead of late-in-gestation, which is the version worth having.

The last three components are a trap of a different kind. They hold 19, 17 and 16 percent, which are close enough to call equal, and when eigenvalues are equal their directions are not determined: any rotation inside that space fits the data exactly as well. In this simulated dataset they are equal by construction, because each fetus was given a shared size factor plus independent noise of the same size on every measurement. The loadings printed for PC2, PC3 and PC4 are therefore a portrait of the random seed. Real data never comes with that fact attached, which is why deciding how many components are real is its own literature and not something the bar chart answers.

Where that leaves us

PCA finds the directions a cloud spreads along and reports how the spread divides among them. Every step of it is honest arithmetic, and none of it knows what the variables mean. It found gestational age because gestational age was the biggest thing in the room, and it named abdominal circumference as the lead variable because abdominal circumference had the biggest numbers. Hand it standardized residuals instead and it answers a different question, correctly, and still says nothing about which question was worth asking.

That has been the pattern for three rungs. Rung 1 fit a straight line through a curve and reported it with the same confidence as any other fit. Rung 2 produced a covariance that moved by a factor of ten thousand when the units changed. Rung 3 rediscovered gestational age. The method never fails, and choosing what to ask it is the part that is not automatic.
